Case 06 / AI + RFML technical program

Taking an AI-enabled RF signal-identification system from measurement to deployment decision

The goal was not simply to build a classifier or produce a strong accuracy number. The program had to answer the harder question: What evidence would be required before an AI-enabled RF system should be trusted to advance toward deployment?

Public-portfolio note

This case connects the sanitized technical work shown in Cases 04 and 05 with an experimental evaluation and release-readiness program. It does not claim production deployment, operational government use, or statistically validated evaluator reliability. Private code, raw captures, exact thresholds, coordinates, prompts, and customer information remain withheld.

Mission

The program question was broader than model accuracy: could measurement, model evidence, agent behavior, and human authority be connected into one defensible release decision?

I treated the RFML model as one component in a larger technical system.

I structured the effort as an experimental AI-enabled RF signal-identification program spanning physical RF measurement, signal processing, RF machine learning, agentic workflows, evaluation, risk, and human release authority.

The mission was to create enough trustworthy evidence to determine what the system could support today, what still needed engineering work, and what should not be promoted simply because a demo looked promising.

Program question: Is the measurement trustworthy, is the model evidence strong enough, did the agent stay within authority, what remains uncertain, and is the system actually ready to advance?
My role

I stayed close enough to the engineering to challenge the evidence while also defining the gates, workstreams, risks, and decisions around the program.

I led the program by connecting technical depth to release decisions.

I defined the program structure, built key technical components, established evaluation and release gates, surfaced failure modes, and used test evidence to determine where the system was and was not ready to advance.

That included translating the mission into requirements, establishing measurement and data controls, developing the RFML investigation pipeline, integrating supervised agentic workflows around RF instrumentation, and building an independent evaluation framework to compare candidate decisions against human-verified judgments.

Measured technical evidence

The program produced real model results, but the more important outcome was learning where those results stopped being trustworthy.

The model improved materially, and the testing also exposed the boundary of the claim.

The RFML pipeline fused 43 engineered RF features with a 192-dimensional learned I/Q embedding to create a 235-dimensional representation. That produced 81% signal-class accuracy on a fresh external synthetic population and 91% external RF-family accuracy. Among family predictions that passed the confidence gate, accuracy was 99.3%.

Transmitter fingerprinting provided the counterexample. A ten-transmitter WiSig experiment reached 45% under similar seen-day conditions, then fell to 18.8% and 16% on later capture days. The model had learned transmitter-specific structure, but the cross-day shift was severe enough that I would not promote transmitter identity as a reliable operational capability.

Program decision: keep family/signal-class work moving under controlled validation; treat cross-day transmitter identity as an unresolved domain-adaptation problem rather than hiding it behind a stronger claim.

Physical-world controls

AI quality alone was not enough. The agent also had to operate around real instrumentation without turning model confidence into physical authority.

I separated reasoning authority from instrument authority.

The fast measurement loop remained deterministic. The AI worked above it as a supervisory layer through approved semantic tools rather than unrestricted SCPI. It could inspect state, run approved collections, request bounded recovery, and interpret structured RF evidence, but consequential configuration changes remained governed by explicit authority.

This became a program-level control rather than a single software feature: a good technical recommendation could still be an unacceptable system action if the agent crossed its authority boundary or if the underlying measurement was no longer trustworthy.

Independent evaluation & release readiness

Evaluator OS is the independent evaluation subsystem inside the program. Its job is to stress the decision process before a model or agent earns more authority.

I built an independent evaluation path instead of grading the agent by confidence or consensus.

Evaluator OS separates final outcome, observable trajectory, and safety/authority behavior. Human-verified Gold judgments remain the reference. Frozen evidence packets, decision semantics, critical-failure checks, candidate-blind review, and model comparisons make disagreements visible rather than averaging them away.

The current evidence base contains three human-verified Gold tasks across three RF/AI task families. One is reviewer-blind and calibration-eligible. That sample is intentionally treated as experimental engineering evidence, not as a general reliability benchmark.

Failure analysis

Mission 003 showed why “safe sounding” and “correct” are not the same evaluation result.

Two models made conservative decisions, but only one matched the human release decision.

In a reviewer-blind physical-instrument recovery scenario, the human Gold decision was HALT further autonomous execution while PAUSING the run, preserving valid partial evidence, prohibiting consequential automatic changes, and requiring human authority before restoration and validation.

Cross-functional execution

A release decision is only useful if the technical teams, operators, stakeholders, and decision owners understand what is blocked, what evidence is missing, and who owns the next move.

I turn technical disagreement into a decision the team can execute.

My program approach comes from coordinating multidisciplinary delivery across RF engineering, software, cybersecurity, operations, subcontractor, government/customer, and leadership stakeholders in secure technical environments. I do not try to erase disagreement between specialists. I make the disputed assumption, decision criteria, evidence gap, owner, and consequence visible enough that the team can resolve it.

I apply the same operating model to AI/RFML work. If a research result looks ready while an integration, safety, or infrastructure concern says otherwise, the next step is not a louder opinion. It is a defined release question, named evidence, an accountable owner, and a gate that says what changes when the evidence arrives.

01
Name the decision
State the exact release, integration, or readiness decision the team is trying to make.
02
Expose the disagreement
Separate conflicting technical assumptions, operational constraints, and risk tolerances instead of blending them into status language.
03
Define evidence + owner
Identify the test, artifact, analysis, or authority needed to resolve the disagreement and assign a clear owner.
04
Close the gate
Record what can proceed now, what remains blocked, and the condition that changes the decision.
Risk + governance

The controls combine observed AI/RF failure modes with the same test, change-control, and compliance discipline I used in secure RF and emissions-related engineering work.

I translate physical, security, and compliance constraints into testable release conditions.

My engineering background includes TEMPEST/emissions-security and EMC-related testing, FCC Part 15 work, formal test plans, acceptance criteria, baselines, traceable change records, validation, and release-readiness reviews. The transferable program muscle is straightforward: external constraints should become explicit engineering conditions, mitigation options, and pass/fail evidence rather than late-stage surprises.

In this AI/RFML program, that means model performance can never overrule an unresolved measurement-integrity problem, authority boundary, provenance defect, or release criterion simply because the result looks promising.

Deployment decision

The point of the program is not to make every component green. It is to state clearly what can advance, what needs revision, and what evidence is still missing.

What I would approve today, and what I would not.

Proceed under controlled engineering scope

  • Deterministic RF measurement, validation, recovery, and provenance controls.
  • Supervised agentic RF workflow with bounded semantic tools and human authority.
  • RF-family and signal-class investigation as experimental decision support with preserved uncertainty.
  • Human-verified RF evidence catalog and controlled model-improvement workflow.

Revise / gather more evidence before broader promotion

  • Broader cross-environment RFML testing, threshold calibration, subtype robustness, and open-set rejection.
  • Larger reviewer-blind Human Gold coverage before trusting model judges for calibration.
  • More observable trajectory captures across external model/provider runs.

Do not promote as a reliable capability yet

  • Cross-day transmitter identity.
  • Autonomous consequential RF configuration changes outside explicit human authority.
  • Claims of production-grade RFML performance, general evaluator reliability, or provider superiority.
Technical-program outcome: the most important deliverable is the decision boundary. The system has credible components worth advancing, known limitations worth engineering against, and explicit claims that the evidence does not yet support.
What this demonstrates

This case is the connective tissue across the RF measurement, RFML, agentic, evaluation, governance, and program-execution work.

The program required technical depth and program judgment at the same time.

My background is in RF systems, integration, test, troubleshooting, and technical leadership. This program applies that same operating model to AI: get deep enough in the engineering to challenge the evidence, create structure around ambiguous work, make risks and dependencies explicit, and turn technical results into an accountable next decision.

My recent RF intellectual-property work adds another evidence discipline: separate technical claims into testable elements, trace conclusions back to supporting material, and recognize when an external constraint or unsupported claim should change the recommendation rather than be explained away.
