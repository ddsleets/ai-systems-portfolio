Case 05 / RFML + persistent RF memory

Building an AI RF investigator that learns from what it measures

Case04 taught the AI how to participate in an RF measurement workflow. Case05 gave it memory: deterministic signal analysis, RF machine learning, hierarchical recognition, uncertainty handling, similarity search, and a controlled path from human-verified captures to future model improvement.

The question

Once an AI can supervise an RF measurement, the next challenge is whether it can recognize what it measured, remember prior evidence, and know when it is uncertain.

Measurement automation was only the first half of the problem.

A useful RF investigator needs to answer more than “did the capture complete?” It needs to ask what signal family is present, whether the subtype is known, whether the observation resembles prior verified captures, whether an emitter hypothesis is supportable, and whether another measurement would materially improve the answer.

I kept the language model outside the sample-rate loop. Deterministic DSP and RFML services perform the numerical work; the AI agent orchestrates the investigation, evaluates evidence and confidence, requests approved follow-up measurements, and hands ambiguous conclusions to the engineer.

The operating loop: measure → analyze → recognize → compare → assess confidence → request more evidence when useful → human verify → learn.
Architecture

The AI manages the investigation. Deterministic software owns signal processing, model execution, evidence storage, and the rules around ground truth.

I separated RF evidence generation from AI reasoning.

Why the separation matters

The LLM never interprets raw I/Q directly and never becomes the real-time acquisition loop. It reasons over structured evidence produced by deterministic RF processing and versioned models.

Signal recognition

I tested multiple representations instead of assuming that a deeper model would automatically outperform RF-domain feature engineering.

The strongest classifier combined RF engineering with learned I/Q features.

I built a 43-feature deterministic representation covering amplitude behavior, normalized complex moments, phase-difference statistics, autocorrelation, spectral entropy, flatness, occupied bandwidth, symmetry, and related RF characteristics. I then trained a 1D CNN to learn a separate 192-dimensional representation directly from normalized I/Q windows.

The standalone raw-I/Q network reached 61% external signal-class accuracy. The best result came from fusing the two views into a 235-dimensional engineered + learned representation.

A compact representation learned directly from signal windows by a convolutional neural network.

Hierarchy + uncertainty

A flat classifier always wants to pick a known answer. An RF investigation system needs a legitimate way to say the family is known while the exact subtype is not.

UNKNOWN became part of the architecture, not a failure state.

An unseen OFDM or PSK subtype should not automatically be treated as a completely novel RF family. I therefore moved from flat classification to a hierarchy: identify the RF family, decide whether that family is supportable, then classify the subtype and make a second acceptance decision.

If the evidence is insufficient or novel, the system returns UNKNOWN FAMILY instead of forcing a match.
S

Within a supported family, the system evaluates the specific modulation or signal subtype.
?

An unfamiliar subtype can remain UNKNOWN SUBTYPE while retaining the useful family-level conclusion.

Transmitter fingerprinting

Signal classification asks what the waveform is. Fingerprinting asks which transmitter produced it, which is much more sensitive to the capture environment.

WiSig exposed the difference between learning a fingerprint and trusting it across changing conditions.

I used a controlled ten-transmitter slice of the WiSig ManyTx benchmark and deliberately made the test harder than a random packet split: the model trained on the first two capture days and was evaluated on later days from the same receiver.

The initial chronological model reached 22% accuracy on the untouched fourth day versus a 10% random baseline. A follow-up diagnostic separated same-day learnability from cross-day generalization.

16%

The same pattern persisted on the second chronological holdout day, exposing substantial domain drift.

Engineering conclusion: the model learned transmitter-specific information, but the observed fingerprint changed enough across days that a practical system cannot treat nearest-transmitter classification as ground truth. Emitter identification needs confidence, historical evidence, repeated observations, and human verification.
RF memory

The long-term value is not a one-shot classifier. It is a persistent evidence system that can compare a new capture against what has already been verified.

Each verified observation can become part of a searchable RF memory.

Raw signal files remain immutable and external to the catalog. The local database stores capture identity, metadata, feature versions, embeddings, model versions, predictions, evidence relationships, human labels, and validation state. That lets the AI ask not only “what did the model predict?” but also “what verified captures are most similar, what conflicts with this hypothesis, and which model produced the result?”

ID

RF

AI
Model provenance

Predictions never become ground truth automatically. Verified labels are recorded separately.

Controlled learning

The system can get better over time, but only through a reviewable promotion path.

I designed the learning loop to avoid teaching the model from its own mistakes.

01
Candidate label
The model proposes a family, subtype, or emitter hypothesis with confidence and supporting evidence.
02
Human verification
The engineer accepts, corrects, or leaves the observation unresolved.
03
Training snapshot
Only eligible verified labels are assembled into a versioned candidate training set.
04
Candidate model
Training creates a new candidate rather than silently replacing the active model.
05
Validation + approval
The candidate must pass defined validation and a human promotion decision before becoming an approved model.
What I learned

The useful outcome was not a single benchmark number. It was understanding which parts of the RF problem need physics, learned representations, uncertainty, memory, and human judgment.

The strongest architecture was hybrid, hierarchical, and explicit about uncertainty.

More data mattered before more complexity.Increasing the reference population produced a major jump in closed-set signal recognition before deeper models were introduced.
RF physics and learned features were complementary.The confirmed winner fused engineered RF evidence with a learned I/Q representation rather than relying on either alone.
UNKNOWN is operationally useful.A trustworthy RF investigator needs to preserve uncertainty rather than forcing every observation into a known class.
Hierarchy preserves partial knowledge.The system can retain a correct RF-family conclusion even when the exact subtype is unfamiliar.
Emitter fingerprints drift.The WiSig benchmark showed that transmitter-specific information can be learnable while still degrading sharply across changing capture days.
Verification creates knowledge.Model outputs are hypotheses; human-verified evidence is what becomes trusted RF memory and future training material.
Back to the FieldFox

The benchmark work was built to support the physical-world system, not replace it.

The next real-world step is verified FieldFox I/Q flowing through the same evidence architecture.

The operating concept is straightforward: the AI identifies an RF question, the FieldFox performs an approved measurement, deterministic DSP and RFML generate evidence, the system searches prior verified captures, and the agent decides whether it has enough support or whether another measurement is worth taking.

Case04 + Case05: supervised RF measurement control plus persistent RF recognition, confidence, evidence search, and a human-verified learning loop.

That is the foundation I wanted to demonstrate: not an AI chatbot connected to a spectrum analyzer, but a governed AI-assisted RF investigation system that can measure, recognize, remember, challenge its own confidence, and improve from verified engineering evidence.
